# The Seasons of Prayer

I love Christmas. It is, like for many, one of my favorite holidays. The build-up, the excitement, the joy on my child's face, it all is amazing for me. But for many, and it has been this way for decades now, feel that Christmas is vapid, full of meaningless things where we spend a lot of money, burn a hole into our wallets, drink and party a little too much, and then become too exhausted to turn around and work on the 26th or the 27th.

However, for another great many, myself included, Christmas is actually just the start.

Holidays are derived from the words "Holy Days" and there are in fact Twelve of them at Christmas time. For ancient christians, it was twelve days of celebration, not one, then a cheese-filled blur of the 26th-January 3rd. Rather it was 12 days of feasting, celebrating, rejoicing in God and celebrating togeter as friends and family.

There are a great many more Holy Days throughout the year, far more than we might be aware of. It can be for apostles, martyrs, leaders, teachers, and good people. These days might include merryment (like St. Valentines' day or St. Patrick's Day) or may simply include an extra day to go to church (like St. Peter's and Paul's day). However, some Holy Days are more important.

Days like Ascension Day, Pentacost Day, Annuciation Day, Visitation Day, Holy Cross Day, Transfiguration Sunday, Trinity Sunday, All Saint's Day and All Soul's Day are important, spiritual days that anchor us to the story of Jesus, the traditions of days gone by, and give us a chance to slow down and contemplate the nature and meaning of God, rejoice in things that are gone, and things that are good, or give us a chance to recommit ourselves to being "at one" with Jesus and our Heavenly Father.

If you look at the calendar section of this book, you will see that some days are the most important. These include the life of jesus, his birth, his death, his ressurection, and for LDS folk, the restoration and His appearance to Joseph Smith.

I've included important moments in the restoration and from your faith as part of the calendar for a number of reasons:

1. You have a history unique to the rest of christianity
2. You have events that are worth remembering and/or celebrating
3. It will help you learn and know your own history, its complexity and this its importance.
4. Who doesn't want a feast day surrounding the First Vision?

## How to Celebrate Holy Days and Seasons

Not every day needs to be some grand event like Christmas or Easter. Sometimes, they are simple mentions of the person in a prayer. Other days might mean going to church out of obligation or desire. For example: Most people don't give gifts on St. Peter's and St. Paul's day, but they might give some on the Feast of the Birth of Mary (Marymas).

How you go about handling each season and feast day will look different for each person and each family, but here are some suggestions for each of the different seasons and holy days I present below.

## The Liturgical Season and Holy Days

### Advent

Our Liturgical Year starts on the First Sunday of Advent, the Sunday that falls on or closest to 30 November and it ends on the Evening Prayer of Christmas Eve, at which point we are in Christmastide.

Advent is often misunderstood as "The Christmas Season" and this is incorrect. This pushing up of christmas into Advent is a result of modern consumerist culture that began in the 1950's. Prior to the second world war, Advent was still widely remembered and reconized culturally, although in the USA, this has changed significantly.

Advent isn’t also just about Christ birth, but it’s also about his second coming. Advent serves a dual purpose. While the primary purpose is to prepare oneself for Christmas spiritually, the same preparation we would do for such a day can also be applied to preparing for the second coming one might argue that the second coming is the Christmas to end all Christmases. 

Therefore, throughout the prayers, the scripture readings homilies. There is much talk about preparing oneself for the holidays as there is to prepare one soul for Christ, try on full return


#### How you can celebrate it

**Advent Sundays**: There are 4 Sundays in Advent, with the last sometimes falling on Christmas Eve, but never on Christmas. These sundays are special and have themes to them:

- *First Sunday*: Hope
- *Second Sunday*: Faith
- *Third Sunday*: Joy
- *Fourth Sunday*: Love/Peace

*O Antiphons*: From December 17th through December 23rd, there will be special spoken phrases called Antiphons that are recited as a part of either Morning or Evening Prayer. These seven antiphons are called the "O Antiphons" and make up the basis of the seven verses of "O Come O Come Emmanuel." They are as follow:

    17 December: O Sapientia (O Wisdom)
    18 December: O Adonai (O Lord/O God)
    19 December: O Radix Jesse (O Root of Jesse)
    20 December: O Clavis David (O Key of David)
    21 December: O Oriens (O Dawn of the East)
    22 December: O Rex Gentium (O King of the Nations)
    23 December: O Emmanuel

You can find the text in Part 2 of this book as well as how to use them.

**Advent Wreaths**: Advent Wreaths have 4 to 5 spaces for candles. An Additional one is lit each Sunday until christmas, when a 5th and final candle may be lit. I have fond memories growing up of fighting over who would get to light the advent candle for that Sunday dinner. In Part 2 is a small litany you could use when liting the candle

**Fasting**: Prior to 1917 at the latest, it was common to fast like in the season of lent below throughout the advent season, however after the Reformation, the rules on fasting relaxed and became a personal choice of preference. However, Advent is still held as a season of repentence, preparation, and slowing down.

**Decorate Slowly**: Instead of rushing to get decorations up (which will be up with a lot of time to admire and love them, as you will see below), decorate slowly, first with some lights, and a bare tree, then over the course of each

**Lots of Purple (or light blue)**: The color of the Advent season is purple or light blue. You may choose to incorporate it as a part of your at-home worship space, or you may opt to not use it.

Lessons and carols comes from the church of England, and something specifically that the church of England does every year posted by the archbishop of Canterbury at Westminster, carols our son which dinner in interspersed by lessons, read by prominent members of the community or the nobility. #### Twelfth Night (Epiphany Eve)

The 5th of January is the last day of Christmas and is often the center of celebrations as much as Christmas. Depending on local customs and traditions, decorations are taken down this night, carols are sung and lots of cultures eat some varation of a King's Cake.

It is so fitting that these twelve days are so holy, only to be followed by racous celebration (maybe too racous in too many regards.)

From this point, the natural order of things has been overturned. We humans now rest, the King is here (and will come again). The First shall be last and the Last shall be First. Our belief in the social heirachy has been suspended. 

It is a time of celebration, love, service, giving, community, gathering. The rich serve the poor, the poor become billionaries and princes, and, when done right, we can glimpse heaven in our fellow brothers and sisters.

This period, called Ephiphanytide, in some places marks the start of Carnival (literally "carne vale" or "goodbye meat"). Either way, it the end of the High Holy Days of Christmas and the start of our long period of celebration and rejoicing.

Traditions also include the Chalking of the Doors, Giving Gifts, Weekly parties and remembering the wise men who visited Jesus bearing gifts

### Pre-Lent: Catachesis, Carnival, and Shrove Tuesday

In the 3 weeks leading up to Lent, the church begins preparing for Lent, a season of repentence, reflexion and preparation. During this time, certain places, like Venice, might begin their Carnival, a celebratory period before the penitential one.

Within the church, those who are preparing for baptism and confirmation would start at this point or at the least, increase in their tempo and depth of preparation.

#### The 3 Sundays (Western Church)

These traditionally have latin names, but are simply the numbers for 70, 60, and 50. Each features a parable and a Psalm as their theme:

- 70th Sunday:  Parable of the Workers in the Vineyard, Psalm 18
- 60th Sunday:  Parable of the Sower, Psalm 44
- 50th Sunday:  Jesus Heals the Blind Man at Jericho, Psalm 31

These are still in use by most of the Eastern Church and are as follow:

- Zacceus Sunday
- Publican and Phrasee Sunday
- Prodigal Son Sunday

A hallmark of this period is preparation for fasting, contemplation, and recommitment.

#### Mardi Gras, Shrove Days

Starting the weekend before Ash Wednesday, Carnival will be in full swing. Likewise, in the anglo-sphere, Shrove Sunday, Monday and Tuesday are days for consuming meats, fats, dairy, and other "sinful" foods, not to eat in excess (though many do), but instead to use them up and no longer have them in the house.

It is, or should be, a period of confession of sins to a priest and to contemplate what one will give up for the Lenten season. For some, the tradition of eliminating meat and alcohol is common, while others seek to give up vices with the support of others.

On top that, this is the last opprotunity for merrymaking and over-the-top celebration until Easter and as a result, Mardi Gras became popular.

### Lent

In the western church, Lent is marked by the start of ash wednesday and either ends on Maundy Thursday or on Holy Saturday, though fasting continues through to the evening of Holy Saturday. 

Regardless of the tradition and background, the 3 core themes throughout lent are the following: 

1. prayer (justice toward God)
2. fasting (justice toward self)
3. almsgiving (justice toward neighbours)

**Fasting**

Fasting is a well-known hallmark of Lent and how you personally choose to approach it it entirely up to you. You may choose to abstain from meats on Sundays, Wednesday, and Fridays, except on particular feast days. Your fast might be positive, incorporating something good into your life, rather than fully excluding something bad.

Some Christians might follow the practice also found in Ramadan, where, from sun up to sun down, no food or drink is consumed, and any food eaten is veggitarian. Some people undertake a "Daniel Fast": No meat, fish, egg, dairy products, chocolates, ice creams, sugar, sweets, wine or any alcoholic beverages (That's easy for you, hard for Anglicans and Catholics!)

Treat this time as a large, 40-day Fast Sunday. Like with fasting, the money you would have spent on food can be donated in a fast offering or a charitable act.

For me, St. Augustine's Prayer Book, a companion to the Book of Common Prayer that I use, states that fasting is "usually meaning not more than a light breakfast, one full meal, and one half meal, on the forty days of Lent." Of course, depending on health and circumstances, this may be adjusted to ensure that a person remains healthy.

How you choose to approach fasting is personal and should be meaningful for you.

### Pentacost

#### Restoration

Starting on the 24th with the Nativity of St. John the Baptist, we have 10 days of celebration leading up to either St. Thomas Day (Modern) or 4th of July, called Indepenence Day in the USA. For those not in the USA, this celebration would end on St. Thomas Day, rather than on 4th of July.
